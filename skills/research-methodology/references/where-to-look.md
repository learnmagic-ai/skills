# Where to look: spreading your sources

The main skill is about **checking** what you find. This page is about **where you look in the first place.** The two go together: careful checking of a narrow pile of sources still gives you a narrow answer.

## The big idea: variety beats volume

Running five searches on the same search engine is really one source, five times. Real variety means looking in genuinely **different kinds** of places, which were made differently and fail in different ways.

Six ways sources differ. Aim to cover more than one position on each that matters for your question:

| Difference | One end | Other end |
|---|---|---|
| **How current** | the AI's own memory (frozen at a past date) | today's news and live pages |
| **Whose** | the public web | your own notes, files, emails, past work |
| **How specialised** | general web search | research papers, official records, expert documentation |
| **Which mind** | one AI model | a second AI, or a human expert, with different blind spots |
| **How reachable** | ordinary public pages | pages behind a login or an app |
| **What shape** | articles and opinions | raw data, official registers, tables, records |

## Kinds of sources, from quickest to deepest

1. **The AI's own memory.** Instant and broad, but can be out of date or wrong in the details, and it can't show you where it learned something. Use it for ideas of what to look up, never as proof.
2. **The open web.** News, websites, encyclopaedias. If you can, use more than one search engine: they find different things. When one source matters a lot, read the whole original, not just a summary.
3. **Pages behind a login or inside an app.** If the answer is there, you (the person) may need to open it and paste in the relevant part.
4. **A second opinion from a different mind.** Ask another AI, or better, a person who knows the field. Where they disagree tells you exactly where the answer is uncertain.
5. **Records and raw data.** Official statistics, company registers, court records, the original study's data, archived copies of old web pages (for example the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine). These are harder to spin than articles about them.
6. **Specialist collections.** Research paper databases (such as Google Scholar or PubMed), official documentation for software and products, government publications.
7. **Your own material.** Your notes, past documents, emails, earlier research. Often overlooked, and you may already have the answer. Check here first, and only use what the person chooses to share.
8. **Social media and forums.** Fast and full of first-hand accounts, but also where copies, rumours, and coordinated campaigns spread most. Apply the "echo vs. confirmation" check hardest here.

## Two ways to dig

- **Wide:** ask one question across several kinds of sources at once. Good for seeing the overall shape of an answer and where sources disagree.
- **Deep:** go far into one strong source. Read the full report, check its references, look at its data. Good when one source carries most of the weight.

Choose on purpose. Most good research does a quick wide pass, then goes deep on the two or three sources that matter most.

## A simple routine

1. Check the person's own material first, if they've shared any.
2. For important facts, use at least two independent kinds of source, not two searches on one.
3. For uncertain or disputed claims, get a second mind (another AI or a human expert) and notice where they disagree.
4. If the question is about an organisation or a thing that has official records, look at the records, not just articles.
5. If something is behind a login, ask the person to open it and paste the relevant part.
6. Say which kinds of source you did **not** use, and why.

## In plain chat with no web access

You can still use this map. Instead of looking things up yourself, give the person a short, specific list, for example:

> To check this, it would help to look at: 1) the original study (search its title on Google Scholar), 2) one news report from a different country, 3) the organisation's own annual report. Paste what you find and I'll check it.

Then run the main skill's checks on what they bring back.
